1. Theory

1.1 What is a hydraulic jump?

A hydraulic jump is a phenomenon in open channel flow where high-velocity (supercritical) water transitions abruptly into slower-moving (subcritical) flow. The water surface rises suddenly, turbulent waves and a rolling roller form, and a large share of the flow’s mechanical energy is dissipated. De Padova and Mossa (2021) put it compactly: a jump occurs whenever an upstream supercritical flow is forced to become subcritical.

A stationary jump sits at a fixed location. Upstream, the flow is fast and shallow. Downstream, it is slow and deep and typically turbulent and choppy. In the transition zone the water slows and deepens in an abrupt step or standing wave.

1.2 A short history

  • Leonardo da Vinci noticed the phenomenon, but Giorgio Bidone was the first to study it experimentally and analytically (De Padova & Mossa, 2021).
  • Bidone published the first experimental investigations in 1820. He described a stream in a rectangular channel held back by a lowered gate, rising abruptly against the gate to form an “intumescence”, meaning a swelling.
  • Jean-Baptiste Bélanger formulated the first modern theory in 1841. His conjugate-depth equation is derived in section 3.

1.3 Where we see it

  • Naturally: where a channel goes from steep to mild slope, and where a stream flows into a still body of water such as a lake.
  • By design: below weirs, spillways and sluice gates, inside stilling basins.
  • In civil engineering: water flowing down a dam spillway carries enough kinetic energy to erode the streambed and undermine the structure. A jump dissipates much of that energy, so it should occur on an apron engineered to withstand the hydraulic forces and to resist cavitation and other erosion (Wikipedia, Hydraulic jump).
  • In drainage: channels, culverts, partly full pipes, steep-to-mild transitions and outlet structures (CivilGEO, 2026). Jumps also matter for flood management and fish-passage design.
  • At home: the tap-on-sink ring. Water spreads radially, fast and thin, then jumps at a critical radius to a deeper, slower depth that conserves momentum. The ring moves in or out as the flow rate changes. This is a circular jump; our derivation is for a straight, horizontal rectangular channel (Hewett, 2025).

1.4 Flow regimes and the Froude number

Flow conditions in an open channel are classified by the Froude number, a dimensionless ratio of inertial to gravitational forces. In a rectangular channel the hydraulic depth y is just the flow depth.

Fr = V√g·y

Physically, √(g·y) is the speed of a small surface wave, so Fr compares the flow speed with how fast a disturbance can travel. That is why the regimes behave so differently:

  • Supercritical (Fr > 1): fast and shallow. Waves cannot travel upstream, so upstream conditions control the flow.
  • Subcritical (Fr < 1): slow and deep. Waves travel both upstream and downstream, so downstream conditions control the flow.
  • Critical (Fr = 1): the transition between the two states.

1.5 Specific energy and critical depth

Specific energy E is the total mechanical energy per unit weight of fluid, measured from the channel bed. On a horizontal bed it is the depth (pressure head) plus the velocity head. With discharge per unit width q = V·y:

E = y + V²2g = y + q²2g·y²

For a fixed q, E has a minimum. Setting dE/dy = 0 gives 1 − q²/(g·y³) = 0, which defines the critical depth:

yc = ∛q²g    Emin = 32·yc

At yc the velocity head is yc/2, which gives the 1.5 factor, and Fr = 1. For any E above the minimum there are two alternate depths: a shallow, fast supercritical one and a deep, slow subcritical one. In a jump the flow goes from the shallow branch to the deep branch, but at a lower energy, so a jump is not a move between alternate depths.

1.6 Why two depths? The momentum function

What the jump does conserve is momentum. Per unit width, the pressure force plus the momentum flow, divided by ρg, is the momentum function:

M(y) = y²2 + q²g·y

M also has a minimum at yc (dM/dy = y − q²/(g·y²) = 0 gives the same yc). For any M above that minimum there are two depths with the same momentum, called conjugate depths: the initial depth y1 (supercritical branch) and the sequent depth y2 (subcritical branch). This is exactly what the M–y graph in the simulator shows.

Increasing q moves the whole M curve to the right and up, giving the flow more momentum at its critical point. At the same M, a larger q means a larger y1 and a smaller y2, so the two depths move closer together. Since the energy loss scales with (y2 − y1)³ (section 3), a smaller depth gap means less energy lost.

1.7 Where the energy goes

In the experiment, rapid flow enters from the left and slows toward the right. Some kinetic energy becomes potential energy as the water gains depth, but most of the difference is lost: the turbulent roller converts mechanical energy into heat (and some sound). For the control volume between section 1 (upstream) and section 2 (downstream), Hewett (2025) writes the energy balance as

y₁ + V₁²2g = y₂ + V₂²2g + Δe

where Δe is the head loss. The loss depends on the two conjugate depths, but those depths are themselves fixed by the flow (q and y1), so velocity still matters indirectly.

  • Undular: smooth, oscillatory waves propagate downstream and the rise in surface elevation is gradual.
  • Weak: a smaller rise in surface elevation and less downstream turbulence; a milder steady jump.
  • Oscillating: regular fluctuations in surface elevation and flow, cycling between supercritical and subcritical behavior.
  • Steady: an abrupt rise, turbulent waves and substantial dissipation. This is the ideal and prevalent range for stilling basin design.
  • Strong: a higher rise and more turbulent downstream flow than a steady jump.
The Penn State case study calls 4.5–9.0 the best range, since the large dissipation gives a well-balanced steady jump. It calls 2.5–4.5 the worst, because the large waves can potentially cause structural damage. These ranges come from USBR and are a guide, not exact cut-offs: near 4.5 or 9 a jump shows features of both neighbors.

1.9 Tailwater and what keeps a jump in place

A jump forms only where flow enters supercritical (Fr₁ > 1) and leaves subcritical (Fr₂ < 1). It holds its position when the downstream depth, the tailwater, matches the sequent depth y2. If the tailwater is too low, the jump is swept downstream. If it is too high, the jump moves upstream and can drown. Engineers use this to size stilling basins and outlet protection.

Sources: CivilGEO (2026), De Padova and Mossa (2021), Hewett (2025), Penn State, Wikipedia, USBR (Martin & Wagner, 1961).

2. Experiment overview

We model the classic flume experiment (Hewett, 2025). Water flows steadily from left to right: rapid, shallow flow enters on the left, passes through a turbulent roller, and leaves slower and deeper on the right. The two sections of interest are section 1 (upstream, depth y1) and section 2 (downstream, depth y2), at the same bed level (z1 = z2). The energy line drops by ΔE across the jump; finding ΔE is one goal of the derivation.

The depths y1 and y2 are called conjugate or sequent depths. The jump is a natural energy dissipator: the same flow that arrives with a lot of kinetic energy leaves with much less.

3. Derivation

3.0 Control volume, assumptions and symbols

Draw a control volume from section 1 to section 2 around the jump. We assume (1) a horizontal rectangular channel, so the weight component along the flow is negligible; (2) steady, one-dimensional flow before and after the jump; (3) hydrostatic pressure, P = ρ·g·h; and (4) negligible bed friction across the short jump.

3.1 Continuity

In a steady channel of constant width b, the same q enters at section 1 and leaves at section 2:

q = V₁·y₁ = V₂·y₂  ⟹  V₁ = qy₁   and   V₂ = qy₂

3.2 Momentum equation

With hydrostatic pressure, the pressure force per unit width at a section of depth y is

F = ∫₀y ρ·g·s ds = 12·ρ·g·y²

Momentum principle: net force = momentum flow out − momentum flow in. The mass flow per unit width is ρq, so

12ρg·y₁² − 12ρg·y₂² = ρq(V₂ − V₁)

Divide by ρg and collect section 1 on the left and section 2 on the right:

y₁²2 + q·V₁g = y₂²2 + q·V₂g

3.3 Substitute velocity and check the units

Use V = q / y from continuity. Each side becomes

q·V₁g = q·(q/y₁)g = q²g·y₁    and    q·V₂g = q²g·y₂

so the momentum balance is M(y₁) = M(y₂), with

y₁²2 + q²g·y₁ = y₂²2 + q²g·y₂    i.e.   M(y) = y²2 + q²g·y

Why q², not q³? q has units m²/s, g has m/s² and y has m. So q²/(g·y) = (m⁴/s²)/(m²/s²) = m², which matches the hydrostatic term y²/2 (m²). A q³ term would have units m⁴/s and could not be added to m². Each term contains exactly one q from the momentum flow ρq and one from V = q/y, giving q·q = q².

Why not Bernoulli? Bernoulli assumes no energy loss along a streamline, but a jump is defined by its loss: E₁ > E₂ (Chanson, 2022). Momentum is conserved across the jump; energy is not. Energy comes in only afterward, to find the loss.

3.4 Solving for the depth ratio (Bélanger equation)

Move the depth terms to one side and use 1/y₁ − 1/y₂ = (y₂ − y₁)/(y₁y₂):

y₂² − y₁²2 = q²g·y₂ − y₁y₁·y₂

Factor y₂² − y₁² = (y₂ − y₁)(y₂ + y₁) and divide by (y₂ − y₁), which is nonzero in a jump:

y₁ + y₂2 = q²g·y₁·y₂  ⟹  q²g = 12·y₁·y₂·(y₁ + y₂)   (★)

Introduce Fr₁² = q² / (g·y₁³) and let r = y₂ / y₁. Dividing (★) by y₁³:

Fr₁² = r(1 + r)2  ⟹  r² + r − 2Fr₁² = 0  ⟹  r = −1 ± √1 + 8·Fr₁²2

The negative root gives a negative depth, so we keep the positive root:

y₂y₁ = 12( √1 + 8·Fr₁² − 1 )

Checks: Fr₁ = 1 gives y₂/y₁ = 1 (no jump). For large Fr₁ the ratio grows like √2·Fr₁. Writing the same algebra with Fr₂² = q²/(g·y₂³) gives the reverse relation (Hewett, 2025):

y₁y₂ = 12( √1 + 8·Fr₂² − 1 )

3.5 Energy loss, step by step

Step 1: specific energy. On a horizontal bed (elevation head z = 0):

E = y + V²2g = y + q²2g·y²

Step 2: write the loss. The head loss is upstream minus downstream specific energy. Group the depth terms and the velocity-head terms:

EL = E₁ − E₂ = (y₁ − y₂) + q²2g( 1y₁² − 1y₂² ) = (y₁ − y₂) + q²2g·y₂² − y₁²y₁²·y₂²

Step 3: eliminate q²/g using (★) from the momentum balance:

EL = (y₁ − y₂) + 12·y₁·y₂·(y₁ + y₂)2·y₂² − y₁²y₁²·y₂²

Step 4: simplify. The factors combine to 1/4, and y₁·y₂ in the numerator cancels one factor in the denominator y₁²y₂²:

EL = (y₁ − y₂) + (y₂ + y₁)(y₂² − y₁²)4·y₁·y₂

Factor y₂² − y₁² = (y₂ − y₁)(y₂ + y₁) and write the first term as −(y₂ − y₁):

EL = −(y₂ − y₁) + (y₂ − y₁)(y₂ + y₁)²4·y₁·y₂

Step 5: combine into one fraction. Put the first term over 4y₁y₂ and factor out (y₂ − y₁):

EL = (y₂ − y₁)·((y₂ + y₁)² − 4·y₁·y₂)4·y₁·y₂

Expand the bracket: (y₂ + y₁)² − 4y₁y₂ = y₂² − 2y₁y₂ + y₁² = (y₂ − y₁)². So

ΔE = EL = E₁ − E₂ = (y₂ − y₁)³4·y₁·y₂

3.6 What the equation tells you

  • Direction of flow (second law). Energy can only be dissipated in a passive jump, so ΔE > 0. The denominator 4y₁y₂ is positive, so (y₂ − y₁)³ must be positive: y₂ > y₁. Flow can jump up from supercritical to subcritical, never down.
  • Cubic scaling with jump height. With hj = y₂ − y₁, the numerator is hj³. Doubling the height difference multiplies the numerator by 2³ = 8, which is why tall jumps in stilling basins strip so much destructive energy before water reaches the riverbed. (This holds for the numerator; the depths in the denominator change too.)
  • Where it goes. Momentum is conserved (M₁ = M₂) but energy is not (E₁ > E₂). The swirling roller converts kinetic energy into internal thermal energy and sound.

3.7 Power dissipated, step by step

Step 1: definition. Power is the rate of energy dissipation:

P = ΔEmechΔt

Step 2: relate to head. Head loss is mechanical energy per unit weight W of fluid (J/N = m), so ΔEmech = W·EL.

Step 3: express weight. W = m·g and m = ρ·Vol, so W = ρ·g·Vol = γ·Vol, where γ = ρg is the specific weight (N/m³). Then ΔEmech = γ·Vol·EL.

Step 4: divide by time. In steady flow, Vol/Δt is the volumetric flow rate Q (m³/s):

P = γ·Vol·ELΔt = γ·Q·EL

Step 5: substitute the head loss. With EL from section 3.5 and γ = ρg:

P = ρ·g·Q·(y₂ − y₁)³4·y₁·y₂    and per unit width    Pb = ρ·g·q·(y₂ − y₁)³4·y₁·y₂

Dimensional check: (kg/m³)(m/s²)(m³/s)(m) = kg·m²/s³ = J/s = W.

3.8 Dimensional analysis (Buckingham Pi)

Ignoring viscosity and surface tension, the downstream depth depends on y₂ = f(y₁, V₁, g). That is 4 variables built from 2 dimensions (L, T), so 4 − 2 = 2 dimensionless groups: y₂/y₁ and V₁²/(g·y₁) = Fr₁². Hence y₂/y₁ = f(Fr₁), and the Bélanger equation is that function. Density drops out because every term in the momentum balance carries ρ.

The same logic gives ΔE/y₁ = f(Fr₁), which we can check: with r = y₂/y₁, ΔE/y₁ = (r − 1)³/(4r), a function of Fr₁ alone. Jump length is found by experiment, Lj/y₁ = f(Fr₁); the ≈ 6y₂ estimate used in the simulator is a rough engineering value. At small laboratory scales, viscosity (Reynolds number) and surface tension can add further groups.

What we learned and want to learn next

  • Why momentum, not energy, fixes the conjugate depths, and why the loss grows like (y₂ − y₁)³.
  • AI wording can sound right and be wrong. Example: “loss depends only on conjugate depths, independent of velocity” needed correcting, since the depths are set by the flow.
  • Future work: sloped and non-rectangular channels, tailwater control, and CFD (RANS with VOF) of the roller, validated against Bélanger and measurements.
